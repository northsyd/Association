Media Release / 4 August 2026

Harbour Bridge collision emphasises need for mobile medians.

NSTA is calling for physically separated traffic lanes on the Harbour Bridge, including the potential use of mobile median barriers.

Media Release

4 August 2026

North Sydney Transport Association

A head-on collision on the southern approach to the Sydney Harbour Bridge shortly after 2pm on 4 August injured three people and left only one of the four northbound lanes operating, with congestion extending towards Anzac Bridge and the Domain Tunnel.

The incident follows previous serious collisions on the Harbour Bridge, including fatal crashes in 2024 and 2020. NSTA says the repeated occurrence of head-on collisions highlights the safety challenges associated with the bridge's dynamic lane system.

The Association is particularly concerned about the narrow lanes and the limited physical separation between opposing traffic movements. With vehicles travelling at high speeds, NSTA says the consequences of a driver crossing into an opposing lane can be severe.

“When we're experiencing head-on collisions every few years, it's clear that something needs to change.”

NSTA Vice President Charlie Green said the existing system places significant demands on drivers using the bridge.

“With vehicles passing less than a metre apart, the margin of error is extremely small, and drivers are often so focused on maintaining a safe margin in their lane that they're not simultaneously paying attention to the dynamic lane indicators.”

A physical barrier between opposing traffic

The North Sydney Transport Association is calling for the consideration of mobile median systems, such as the Road Zipper, to physically separate northbound and southbound traffic while retaining the ability to dynamically change lane allocations.

NSTA says a physical median could reduce the risk of vehicles crossing into opposing traffic while allowing the bridge's dynamic lane arrangement to continue operating.

The proposal would also provide a clearer physical indication of the boundary between opposing traffic flows, rather than relying solely on overhead lane indicators.

“We understand this may be a logistical challenge, but in future, the impact crashes have on the area's main cross-harbour corridor will be greatly lessened as a result.”

NSTA President Lachlan Steenson said the Association believes the opening of the Western Harbour Tunnel could create an opportunity to reconsider the allocation of road space on the Harbour Bridge.

“To be clear, by the opening of the Western Harbour Tunnel, capacity required on the bridge will be low enough to be able to reduce a lane and have the space required to implement crash barriers.”

An opportunity after the Western Harbour Tunnel

The Western Harbour Tunnel is expected to open in 2028. According to Transport for NSW, the project is expected to reduce traffic using the Harbour Bridge, with some traffic instead travelling through the WestConnex M4 and M8 network.

NSTA argues that reduced traffic demand on the bridge could provide an opportunity to remove one general traffic lane and use the additional space for a mobile median system while potentially increasing the width and safety clearance of the remaining lanes.

The Association says such an approach would need to balance traffic capacity with safety and reliability, particularly given the Harbour Bridge's importance as a major cross-harbour transport corridor.

Building a more resilient Harbour Bridge

NSTA says improving the physical separation of opposing traffic would form part of a broader effort to make Sydney's most important road corridors safer and more resilient.

With more than 150,000 vehicles and around 80,000 bus passengers travelling across the Harbour Bridge each day, major incidents can have significant consequences for people travelling across the city.

The Association says investment in safer lane arrangements, alongside greater cross-harbour transport capacity, could reduce the impact of future crashes and improve reliability for Sydney commuters.
