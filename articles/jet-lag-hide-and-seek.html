Playing Jet Lag: The Game's Hide and Seek on the Sydney Rail Network

Three teams. Three days. One giant game of hide and seek across Sydney's trains, metro, ferries and buses.

Lachlan Steenson • 21 April 2026

Type

Date

Introduction

Inspired by the popular YouTube series Jet Lag: The Game, members of the North Sydney Transport Association set out to recreate one of the show's most famous formats: Hide and Seek.

Three teams competed across Sydney's public transport network, using trains, metro services, ferries, buses and light rail to reach hiding locations before rival teams attempted to track them down using a series of clues, questions and special powers.

Hiders were permitted to travel to any Sydney Trains, Sydney Metro, Southwest Link or ferry stop, with buses and light rail available as connecting modes. Intercity trains were excluded due to their low frequency and enormous operating area. Hiding zones were defined as a 250-metre radius around a public transport stop.

What followed was three days of strategy, lucky escapes, ferry chases, airport antics and some surprisingly effective detective work across the Sydney transport network.

Day 1: The Macquarie Park speedrun

The game began at Central Station at 9:00am. Hiders received 45 minutes to travel to a destination of their choosing while the seekers remained behind to develop a strategy.

Rather than immediately chasing trains, the seekers attempted to map the maximum area reachable within the time limit. By analysing departures from Central they quickly narrowed the possible hiding area to a rough 20–25 kilometre radius.

A thermometer question from Wynyard split Sydney into northern and southern halves. A radar soon followed. A station platform photograph then dramatically accelerated the search, revealing that the hiders were almost certainly located on the Epping–Chatswood Rail Link.

Within just over half an hour, the possible locations had been narrowed to Macquarie Park or Macquarie University. A photo of a nearby office building eliminated the university, leaving Macquarie Park as the only logical option.

The seekers arrived, identified a distinctive warehouse roof visible from the hiding location and completed the capture.

Team A's first run ended after 2 hours and 10 minutes.

Rhodes, Rydalmere and the great ferry escape

Team B's run began with ambitious plans to hide along the Parramatta River ferry corridor. The original target was Olympic Park Wharf, chosen because of its limited rail connections and relative isolation from the broader network.

Unfortunately, the hiding timer expired before the team could reach the wharf. They initially settled in Rhodes, but quickly recognised the location was vulnerable.

A thermometer question suggested the seekers were closing in, but a perfectly timed Move card changed everything.

With the seekers temporarily frozen at Rhodes Station, Team B escaped via bus to Olympic Park Wharf and boarded a ferry bound for Rydalmere. The service only operated once per hour, making the transfer both risky and extremely effective.

While Team B cruised along the Parramatta River, the seekers spent valuable time searching entirely different sections of Sydney. Even when they eventually suspected a ferry location, uncertainty and poor timing prevented a quick interception.

By the time the seekers finally reached Rydalmere, Team B had accumulated an impressive hiding time of 4 hours and 54 minutes.

What began as a failed attempt to reach Olympic Park Wharf ended as the longest hiding run of the entire game.

Day 2: Parramatta deduction games

The second day began at Parramatta Station.

Team B's seeker strategy relied heavily on airports. By comparing which airport was closest to both teams, large portions of Sydney could be eliminated almost instantly.

A series of questions narrowed the possible locations to a small number of western Sydney stations before a photograph revealed an unexpected twist: the hiders were effectively back where the seekers had started.

The result was a rapid endgame around Parramatta Station and Westfield Parramatta, with clues involving railway infrastructure, nearby roads and Parramatta Library eventually revealing the exact location.

Team C completed their run with a hiding time of 3 hours and 1 minute.

Burwood and the "Strathtation Strathfield" incident

Team A's second hiding run produced one of the funniest moments of the entire competition.

While travelling westward, seekers encountered a well-known bug on Millennium train passenger information displays. The screen combined the next station message with the station name itself, resulting in the memorable announcement:

"Strathtation Strathfield"

Shortly afterwards, a building photograph was instantly recognised by one of the seekers, who happened to be from the same suburb as the hiders.

The location was identified as Burwood, triggering another rapid endgame.

Banksia, Sydney Airport and controversy

Team B's second hiding run nearly became the most controversial of the game.

Originally hiding near Banksia, the team once again drew a Move card at exactly the right moment. This allowed an escape to Sydney Airport before the seekers could close in.

The hiders explored Domestic Airport and briefly considered hiding beyond security screening, before abandoning the idea due to practical constraints and concerns about legality within the game rules.

Instead, they settled inside the P2 airport car park.

As the seekers slowly narrowed the possibilities, suspicion grew that Team B might actually be hiding somewhere within the airport complex. Several photographs were vetoed, while other clues revealed the team's approximate height above ground and visibility towards Wolli Creek.

The day ended with tensions running high and the seekers increasingly convinced that something unusual was happening.

Day 3: The Manly bluff

Team C began the final hiding run of the game needing almost five hours to secure victory.

The seekers immediately deployed their airport strategy, then used a series of deduction questions to determine that the hiders were probably located at a ferry wharf.

A photograph of a large body of water pointed strongly towards either Rose Bay or Manly. A radar from Circular Quay quickly ruled out Rose Bay, leaving Manly as the only logical candidate.

However, a Joker card forced the seekers to waste valuable time on an unnecessary ferry trip.

Eventually, a combination of airport-distance calculations, building photographs and a clue involving the AMP Building narrowed the search to Customs House Library near Circular Quay.

Team C was located inside the library, ending both the run and the game itself.

Conclusion

What began as a simple attempt to recreate a YouTube game evolved into a surprisingly deep exercise in transport geography, strategic planning and local network knowledge.

Over three days, players crossed Sydney by train, metro, ferry and bus, explored suburbs they may never otherwise have visited, and demonstrated just how interconnected the city's transport system has become.

Most importantly, it was incredibly fun.
